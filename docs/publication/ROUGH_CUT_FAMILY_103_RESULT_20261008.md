# 103: does knowing the rough cutting family rescue localization?

Exploratory readout on the **frozen** 101 branches: 64 independently drawn synthetic sections from eight development deformation plans, 14 unchanged candidates per section, no training or new images. The source was committed before reading these numbers (`training/diagnose_rough_cut_family_103.py`). For this diagnostic only, the section's *true* nearest anatomical axis stands in for a researcher correctly saying “mostly coronal”, “mostly horizontal”, or “mostly sagittal”. This is an optimistic supplied constraint, not an inference result or real user study.

All errors are plan-equal mean visible-tissue mapped96 error. With the trained matcher, 17/64 unconstrained winners have a fitted normal assigned to another nearest-axis family. Nevertheless, hard-filtering to the true family **worsens** error on the 63 covered sections by 0.189 mm; the broad 60° cone improves all-case error by only 0.037 mm. One section has no candidate in the named nearest-axis family. A 60° cone permits substantial tilt and covers all sampled cases, but this simple constraint does not repair the nearly 1.9-mm selected-versus-best gap. The dominant problem is choosing the right location and fit *within* or across broad families, not merely identifying coronal versus horizontal versus sagittal.

This does **not** prove that optional orientation information is useless: a learned soft prior, approximate position range, adjacent ordered sections, or electrode observations could carry more information, and this experiment used the existing score without retraining it to honor constraints. It also does **not** prove arbitrary single-section localization impossible. Keep the full-sphere model; do not silently restrict normal angles. For small, damaged, or anatomically repetitive observations, preserve multiple possibilities and request a rough orientation or neighboring section when confidence cannot be validated. [DeepSlice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10514056/) explicitly cautions against single-image use in its coronal workflow, while [QuickNII](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6541252/) supports tilted atlas planes and ordered series. Neither establishes a universal one-slice, all-angle guarantee.
